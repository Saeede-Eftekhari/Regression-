The Best-Fit Line

Ten students reported how many hours they studied and the score they earned. Excel draws one line through these points. This page shows what that line tells us, how Excel chose it, how good it is, and whether its slope is real.

The observed data: 10 students

These are the only actual scores we have. Each one is a dot on the graphs. For any other number of hours, the line gives a prediction (ŷ), not an actual score.

Goal

Many lines could pass through these points. How does Excel pick one?

Every line misses every student by some amount (the residual = actual − predicted). Excel picks the line whose misses, squared and added up, are as small as possible. That total is called SSE. Move the sliders and try to make SSE smaller than Excel's.

Each shaded square has the miss as its side, so its area is the squared miss. SSE is the total area of the squares.

The SSE bowl

The curve shows the smallest SSE you can get for each slope. Your line is the dot. The bottom of the bowl is Excel's slope, 1.964. No other line gets lower.

Why square the misses?

If we only added the misses, positive and negative misses would cancel. A bad line could score 0. Squaring makes every miss count as positive, and it makes big misses count a lot more than small ones.

Excel's shortcut

Excel doesn't try lines one by one. Two formulas land exactly at the bottom of the bowl:

b₁ = SSxy ÷ SSxx = 519.5 ÷ 264.5 = 1.964
b₀ = ȳ − b₁·x̄ = 70.1 − 1.964 × 10.5 = 49.48

The line always passes through the average point (x̄ = 10.5, ȳ = 70.1).

Goal

How much better is the line than just guessing the class average for everyone?

Without hours, our best guess for every student is the average score, 70.1. Each student's distance from that average splits into two pieces: the part the line explains, and the part left over.

R² = SSR ÷ SST = 0.394. About 39% of the spread in exam scores is explained by hours studied. The other 61% comes from other things (ability, sleep, test nerves…).
Typical miss se = √(SSE ÷ (n − 2)) = √(1,568.6 ÷ 8) = 14.0 points. Predictions from this line are usually off by about 14 points.

For each student, total = explained + left over. After squaring, the sums still add up (SST = SSR + SSE) because Excel's line is the least-squares line.

Goal

Our slope is 1.964. Could a slope that big happen just by luck, even if hours had nothing to do with scores?

Hypotheses: H₀: β₁ = 0 (no linear relationship) vs H₁: β₁ ≠ 0. If H₀ were true, the scores could belong to anyone. So shuffle the scores among the students, refit the line, and see what slopes luck produces.

Line for shuffled scoresReal line (slope 1.964)

Slopes from luck alone

That share is the p-value idea: how often luck alone gives a slope at least as far from 0 as ours.

The t test does this with a formula

SE(b₁) = se ÷ √SSxx = 14.0 ÷ √264.5 = 0.861
t = b₁ ÷ SE(b₁) = 1.964 ÷ 0.861 = 2.28
df = n − 2 = 8  ·  p = 2 × P(t ≥ 2.28) = 0.052
Decision at α = 0.05: 0.052 > 0.05, so we fail to reject H₀. Not enough evidence of a linear relationship. It would be significant at α = 0.10. With only 10 students, a moderate pattern is hard to prove.

Same recipe as the earlier t tests: (estimate − 0) ÷ standard error. In Excel's output, read the p-value in the hours row, not the Intercept row.
